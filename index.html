What this is

Grove is about 31.4 acres on NW Timmerman Road in unincorporated Washington County, Oregon, west of Forest Grove. It is two contiguous parcels: a 26.61-acre main parcel and a 4.82-acre parcel directly to the north.

The main parcel is entitled. Between 2022 and 2026 it went through the full Washington County process: drainage-hazard analysis, geotechnical slope review, and a FEMA flood determination. Grading permit GRAD-2505132 and culvert permit BLDG-2514996 were issued in January 2026. That is the step that stops most rural projects, and it is finished.

Carrying cost is low. Both parcels are under Oregon farm-use (EFU) assessment, so the 2026 property tax is about $526 on the main parcel and $38 on the north parcel, roughly $564 a year combined.

Site and grading work is done by Boss PDX, a licensed contractor (CCB 232383) controlled by one of the partners. Development is handled at cost, without an outside contractor's margin, which is a direct saving on the largest near-term expense.

The south boundary fronts OR-6, the Wilson River Highway, the main route from the Portland area to the north Oregon coast. ODOT's 2024 count at the frontage is about 6,200 vehicles a day, roughly 2.2 million a year, with heavier volumes in summer. Clearing the roadside trees opens a long, visible highway frontage, which matters for a charging site or any highway-facing use.

What the land becomes is not fixed. A vineyard is one option. The property also carries an existing Oregon wine brand, Halem Heights (license, distribution, and inventory), which is available if a wine use is pursued. Other uses are listed further down. None is committed.

Why this site works

The usual blockers on a rural charging site are power and permitting. Both are largely in hand here.

Power at the frontage

A three-phase primary distribution line runs on the poles along OR-6 at the property line, served by PGE, with a Gales Creek substation and 115 kV transmission close by. A large service can tap that highway feeder directly, instead of extending single-phase from a side road. Available capacity is confirmed by a PGE load study, the first step.

Access and approvals

A Washington County Type II commercial land-use approval is already in place, with a permitted road and access to the site. Because access runs off the county road, no new state-highway approach is required.

Tax impact

A charging pad covers a small footprint, a fraction of an acre, so the effect on the parcels' EFU farm assessment is negligible.

Build options: under Tesla's program the owner buys the hardware and pays installation while Tesla operates the site for a $0.10/kWh fee, or a third-party charging operator leases the pad and carries the capital. Fast-charge economics reward corridor traffic and a reason to stop, so the frontage pairs naturally with a destination use on the land.

How much power it actually needs

The 1 MW that Tesla quotes for four posts is a ceiling, the draw only if every stall pulls its maximum at the same instant. A V4 cabinet shares power across its posts, so a smaller service still runs the site: with less capacity the cabinet caps and splits the total, and cars charge a little slower only when the site is full. The service can be sized to the traffic rather than to the nameplate.

That is a real lever on cost. A smaller service means a smaller transformer, a cheaper interconnection, and lower monthly demand charges, which are the swing factor in the economics. Sizing the request under 1 MW also keeps it in PGE's standard commercial-service process rather than the formal Large Load Study that a load of 1 MW or more triggers.

Confirming capacity with PGE

Whether the frontage feeder has headroom is the one open question, and PGE has a defined way to answer it. For a load of 1 MW or more, PGE runs a Large Load Study. It opens with an informal Pre-Feasibility check, no agreement and no deposit, the low-cost way to learn whether capacity exists before committing anything. If it looks viable, the Feasibility, System Impact, and Facilities studies follow and price out any feeder or substation upgrades.

A well here would likely run 165 to 300 ft, with a handful of neighbors reaching 450 to 510 ft on the higher ground. Budgeting for drilling, casing, pump, and hookup, plan on roughly $18k to $28k, with a contingency toward $40k if it has to go deep.

Septic

Washington County Environmental Health permits on-site septic directly: a soil evaluation sets the system, then a construction permit, install, and final inspection. The building area sits well away from the drainage hazard overlay, so that is not a constraint on the drainfield. A standard gravity system runs about $10k to $18k; if the soils call for an engineered system (sand filter or pressurized drainfield) plan $20k to $35k with engineering. Existing permits and as-builts, including a commercial system like Apolloni's, are public but pulled by records request rather than an open map.
